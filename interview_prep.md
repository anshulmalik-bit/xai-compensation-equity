# Interview Preparation: XAI Compensation Equity Audits

## Section 1: Core Architectural Decisions

**Why did you use SHAP-adjusted residuals instead of running Oaxaca-Blinder directly on model predictions or raw data?**
If you run Oaxaca-Blinder on raw data directly, you're forced to use a linear OLS regression which completely fails to capture how real compensation works. Salary growth isn't additive; it multiplies. Performance multipliers scale exponentially with job level. If you use a linear model on multiplicative data, it chronically under-fits the high-earners, pushing massive amounts of legitimate structural variance directly into the "unexplained bias" gap, creating a massive false positive. Alternatively, if you run Oaxaca-Blinder directly on the non-linear model's raw `y_pred`, the complex interactions bleed across features and corrupt the decomposition. By routing the non-linear predictions through a SHAP TreeExplainer, I forced the black-box model into an exact, additive mathematical breakdown. Running the OB decomposition purely on those isolated SHAP residuals perfectly separates the legitimate feature differences from the naked bias, getting the accuracy of a non-linear model with the auditability of a linear one.

**If the audit finds an unexplained gap, does that mathematically prove a company is legally discriminating?**
No, it proves the company bears a severe statistical liability risk, but it does not equate to absolute legal proof of explicit discrimination. An "unexplained gap" simply means that the variance in compensation cannot be explained by the observable features we fed into the model. There are often unmeasured, legitimate variables missing from standard HRIS systems—such as historically negotiated base pay from previous employers, highly specific, un-tagged technical certifications, or extended periods of unpaid leave tracking. Those missing variables get dumped into the "unexplained" bucket. However, under the Equal Remuneration Act, the burden of proof shifts to the employer to document those exogenous factors. If they can't quantitatively prove the discrepancy is tied to legitimate merit, the statistical gap stands as a regulatory breach.

**Why predict log salary instead of the raw INR salary?**
Compensation data strictly follows a log-normal distribution, characterized by a massive right-tail skew. A junior analyst makes ₹6L, while a VP makes ₹50L. If a model tries to optimize for Mean Squared Error on raw un-logged salaries, the algorithm becomes entirely obsessed with minimizing the massive absolute errors at the VP level, fundamentally ignoring the calibration of the junior and mid-level bands where 90% of the workforce literally resides. By log-transforming the target variable, we convert exponential salary growth into a linear scale. The model is then forced to prioritize relative percentage errors equally across the entire corporate hierarchy rather than optimizing exclusively for extreme outliers.

**Your architecture mentioned an "IIT Premium." How does your model handle non-linear educational premiums when OLS misses them?**
In an OLS regression, a feature like an "IIT Degree" is just an additive dummy variable—it assumes graduating from a Tier-1 university simply adds a flat ₹2L to your salary, regardless of whether you are a junior developer or a Senior Director. In reality, educational premiums act as compounding multipliers over an employee's career. A non-linear algorithm like LightGBM natively captures these interactions by mathematically splitting the decision tree simultaneously on multiple features (e.g., `If Tier 1 AND If Job Level > 4`). The SHAP explainer then maps those complex, deep-tree interactions perfectly, allowing us to see that the IIT premium isn't a flat number, but a compounding multiplier that aggressively widens at the executive level.

## Section 2: Implementation & Diagnostics

**Why is `career_velocity` negatively correlated with salary in your dataset?**
In standard logic, a "high velocity" employee should make more money. However, in my dataset, `career_velocity` is mathematically defined as `months_since_promotion / job_level`. A mathematically higher value actually indicates *slower* career progression (doing the same job for far longer without a promotion), representing career stagnation. Therefore, a higher numerical `career_velocity` value signifies stagnation, naturally resulting in a heavily negative correlation with raw salary. When plotted on a SHAP dependence chart, the algorithm cleanly identifies this boundary threshold, severely penalizing the predicted compensation for employees who have stagnated at their current job level for excessive durations.

**Your audit found an explained gap of only 3.8%. Usually, this is 40-60%. Why is it so low?**
The 3.8% explained gap is a deliberate consequence of the synthetic data architecture. In a real organization, structural concentration heavily drives the pay gap—women are historically often underrepresented in senior executive bands or high-paying engineering verticals, which naturally produces a large "explained" legitimate gap. In my synthetic dataset, I intentionally generated a workforce where men and women possess nearly identical distributions across `job_level` and `performance_rating`. The only meaningful legitimate variance was localized in `manager_rating` evaluations. Without structural concentration driving the disparity, the decomposition correctly assigns almost the entire disparity strictly to the unexplainable, direct bias penalty I mathematically injected into the baseline equation.

**You mentioned Jensen's inequality causing a discrepancy between expected and measured gaps. What does that mean?**
During data generation, I injected exactly a `-0.25` coefficient penalty into the log salary equation. Mathematically, `1 - exp(-0.25)` is a 22.1% female penalty. But the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition measures gaps based on the arithmetic mean of *exponentiated raw salaries*, returning a 27.3% gap. The 5.2% drift isn't an error; it's a fundamental principle of mathematics called Jensen's Inequality. Simply put, the arithmetic average of exponentiated logs is always strictly greater than the exponentiated average of logs. Because the male salary distribution has higher variance internally alongside higher means, the exponentiation inherently stretches the arithmetic average higher, mathematically widening the raw-space gap beyond the native log-penalty. 

**Why did you restore `NOISE_STD` to 0.135? Why does data variance matter for an audit?**
During my initial executions, I aggressively reduced the synthetic noise to artificially force the model's RMSE metric under 1.5 LPA. However, suppressing noise destroyed the realism of the dataset. If a dataset has artificially tiny variance, every employee's salary cleanly maps to their standard features, practically erasing the inherent human unpredictability caused by out-of-band negotiations and legacy pay scales. Running SHAP on artificially flawless data forces it to under-report the Oaxaca-Blinder unexplained components, fundamentally understating the true extent of the structural bias. Restoring the noise ensured the pipeline accurately evaluated bias against completely realistic, noisy HR compensation environments.

**Your compliance report flagged a 92 Crore remediation cost. Doesn't that look bizarrely high for 10,000 employees?**
It equals roughly 7.7% of the total organizational payroll, which is drastically higher than the 0.5%–2% remediation scale found in real-world audits. This massive cost is absolutely intentional. To stress-test the model's detection bounds, I mathematically injected a massive 28.4% direct pay penalty targeting the entirety of the 40% female workforce. If you underpay 40% of an entire company by 28%, the aggregated missing payroll mathematically scales perfectly to roughly a 7% to 8% total payroll deficit. The 92 Crore figure isn't an algorithmic hallucination; it's the exact, strictly budgeted cost to successfully reverse an astronomical, intentionally simulated compensation disaster.

## Section 3: Deployment & Execution Limitations

**If I connected this pipeline to a live Workday export right now, what would break first?**
The immediate failure point would be the feature cardinality mapping in the Pydantic validation layer. Real-world HRIS systems like Workday do not cleanly export five neat job levels and three standardized exact location nodes. They export hundreds of messy, overlapping custom internal `job_titles`, overlapping `business_unit` definitions, and inconsistently spelled `cost_center` locales. My system utilizes `rapidfuzz` for string matching against canonical arrays, but it assumes a relatively clean taxonomy. Confronted with a raw Workday pipeline, the categorical encoder would explode trying to handle 300 unique one-hot branches unless a massive, manual HR-driven data-warehousing standardization effort preceded the ingestion entirely.

**If you used LightGBM quantiles, couldn't the P05 and P95 lines cross over each other?**
Yes. LightGBM trains the independent quantile models (P05, P50, and P95) entirely separately. Because they do not natively share logic, it is mathematically possible for the P05 lower-bound prediction to predict a higher value than the P95 upper-bound for highly specific outlier combinations. To mitigate this crossing risk, my architecture intentionally relies on stringent hyperparameter tuning, relatively constrained parameter boundaries, and internal early stopping mechanisms. In a true enterprise-grade scenario, this would be swapped to a strictly constrained monotone objective algorithm or a unified deep learning quantile architecture that mathematically enforces non-crossing constraints.

**How would you connect this system into an Affirmative Action compliance framework like the OFCCP?**
This architecture currently strictly flags whether a compensation discrepancy is explained or mathematically unexplained. Integrating it into a US OFCCP Affirmative Action context requires an entirely separate statistical lens: Disparate Impact vs Disparate Treatment. We would have to pivot the logic off the Oaxaca-Blinder means and build a secondary layer deploying a Fisher's Exact Test or a Mantel-Haenszel test specifically mapping categorical promotions and hiring band velocities within structurally-similar groups. The SHAP-residual framework proves compensation bias successfully, but standard AA audits demand a rigorous cohort-level demonstration of adverse selection velocity, requiring an entirely distinct statistical engine evaluating the pipeline before the compensation is even assigned.
